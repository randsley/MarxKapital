\title{MarxKapital: A Computational Framework for Marxian Value Theory\\
with Real Empirical Calibration}
\author{Nigel Randsley}
\date{July 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We present MarxKapital, an open-source Julia package for the mathematical
modeling of Marxian value theory: capital accumulation, the tendency of the
rate of profit, the transformation of labor values into prices of
production, and the sectoral dynamics of expanded reproduction. Beyond the
classical two-department and generalized $N$-department reproduction
schemes, the package implements three complementary mechanisms for coupling
sectoral accumulation through the technical matrix~$A$ --- capital
mobility (conserving aggregate constant capital exactly by construction),
an aggregate realization-pressure heuristic, and an exact per-sector
quantity-rationing scheme solved by fixed-point iteration --- without
recourse to differential-algebraic equation (DAE) solvers. Both
supply-constrained mechanisms --- realization pressure and quantity
rationing --- operate on gross output reconstructed from the model's own
state and technical matrix, not a value-added-type proxy: circulating
constant capital, initially omitted, is recovered from the model's own
cost identity without adding new state, and investment demand is
converted to output-scale units via each sector's capital-output ratio
before the technical matrix is applied to it. The rationing
mechanism's convergence (270 random trials, $N\in\{6,12,19\}$) and its
effect on cross-sector dispersion (50 random economies) are checked
systematically rather than on a single hand-picked example, though not
yet against real per-sector accumulation data, for which no suitable
time series currently exists. We further present a live, reproducible
empirical pipeline that calibrates the model directly from official
European statistical sources (Eurostat and AMECO, via their SDMX REST
APIs), replacing the synthetic or ad hoc calibration common in the
computational Marxian literature, and apply it to Portugal: a 2022
sectoral national-accounts profile (19 sectors, real value added), a
coherent 2020 production-structure calibration (a technical matrix
reconciled exactly against Eurostat's own published output figures,
paired with 2020 labor coefficients rather than mixing vintages), and
an aggregate profit-rate series for 1996--2022, which
ranges $7.9\%$--$15.1\%$ across three deflation conventions we test ---
a real but modest sensitivity, not the dramatic artifact an earlier,
internally inconsistent version of this calibration produced.

That earlier inconsistency is itself part of what we report. Four
defects, each found by auditing rather than assuming correctness and
each disclosed alongside its fix and quantified consequence: a
stock-flow conflation in the model's own output formula (a capital
\emph{stock} was being added directly to annual \emph{flows}); a
depreciation term that broke an algebraic invariance the corrected rate
of exploitation now satisfies exactly, verified directly rather than
asserted; a technical-matrix construction that, on audit, turned out
to be reading the wrong Eurostat dataflow entirely (a trade-margins
satellite table, not the symmetric input-output table), understating
intermediate flows by more than an order of magnitude and explaining an
implausible spectral radius and maximum profit rate reported in earlier
versions of this work; and, in the dynamical model itself, a supply-side
flow that omitted circulating constant capital entirely and an
investment-demand term that applied the technical matrix to expenditure
rather than output, both corrected by reconstructing genuine gross
output from the model's own state at every solver evaluation. We treat
this progression as a contribution in
its own right, alongside the corrected empirical numbers, on the view
that a live statistical pipeline's value lies as much in surfacing and
fixing exactly this kind of error as in the numbers it ultimately
reports.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Empirical and computational work in the Marxian tradition faces a
recurring reproducibility problem: profit-rate and organic-composition
estimates in the literature are frequently produced by bespoke,
non-public spreadsheets or scripts, calibrated to data sources whose
vintage and exact construction are difficult to reproduce
\citep{shaikh2016capitalism,dumenil1993economics}. At the same time,
existing open computational implementations of Marxian reproduction
schemes tend to rely on synthetic or randomly generated sectoral data for
demonstration purposes, since assembling a real technical (input-output)
matrix, real sectoral value-added and employment series, and real capital
stock data from official sources is itself a substantial undertaking.

MarxKapital is an attempt to close both gaps at once. It is, first, a
methodological contribution: a Julia implementation of the classical
two-department reproduction scheme, its generalization to $N$ departments,
the labor-value and Sraffian production-price apparatus for the
transformation problem, and --- the paper's central technical
contribution --- three distinct mechanisms for coupling $N$-department
accumulation dynamics through the technical matrix, culminating in an
exact quantity-rationing scheme in the spirit of the disequilibrium-macro
literature \citep{barro1971general,benassy1975neokeynesian}, implemented
without the numerical machinery a literal differential-algebraic
formulation would require. This last mechanism is validated
numerically and demonstrated on a synthetic economy (Section~5.4); it
has not yet been tested against real per-sector accumulation data. It
is, second, an empirical contribution: a
live data pipeline that fetches real sectoral national accounts,
input-output tables, and macroeconomic series from Eurostat and the
European Commission's AMECO database via their official SDMX REST APIs,
and uses them to calibrate every quantity the model needs --- constant
and variable capital, the rate of exploitation, the organic composition,
the technical matrix itself, and (optionally) a perpetual-inventory
capital-stock series --- with no synthetic fallback unless explicitly
requested.

We use this pipeline to calibrate the model for Portugal and report two
sets of results: a detailed 19-sector profile for 2022, and an aggregate
time series of the rate of exploitation, organic composition, and profit
rate for 1996--2022. An earlier draft of this paper reported the latter
series with a genuine defect: gross output throughout the model,
$O_i=C_i+V_i+S_i$, added a capital \emph{stock} ($C_i$) to two annual
\emph{flows} ($V_i,S_i$), and the aggregate time series used a flat 10\%
of the capital stock as its depreciation deduction rather than reported
data. External review caught both. The stock-flow fix replaces $C_i$ in
every output-facing formula with $\delta_iC_i$, the capital
\emph{transferred} in a period; the depreciation fix replaces the flat
10\% with AMECO's own consumption-of-fixed-capital series. Together they
have a checkable algebraic consequence, which we verify directly: the
rate of exploitation $e^{\text{net}}=(Y-V-\text{CCF})/V$ is now exactly
invariant under a common deflator applied to $Y$, $V$, and CCF together,
as it must be, which it demonstrably was
not under the earlier, buggy formula. With the fix in place, we
re-examine the same valuation question the earlier draft raised ---
whether AMECO's mixed-valuation capital and flow series distort the
measured trend --- and find a real but much more modest effect than
before: Portugal's profit rate ranges from $7.9\%$ to $15.1\%$ across
three deflation conventions over 1996--2022 --- $7.9$--$11.5\%$ unadjusted,
$9.3$--$12.7\%$ GDP-deflated, $9.3$--$15.1\%$ investment-deflated --- moving
mildly in either direction within each convention rather than
dramatically, with the residual sensitivity now
concentrated in the profit rate (through the classical
capital-advanced denominator $C+V$) rather than in the rate of
exploitation. We report the whole sequence --- the bug, the fix, and the
correctly-measured residual sensitivity --- rather than only the final
numbers, since the sequence is itself the more durable methodological
lesson.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section~2 situates the
model in the relevant theoretical literature. Section~3 presents the model
itself, including the coupled $N$-department dynamics and the
quantity-rationing mechanism. Section~4 describes the empirical
calibration pipeline and the Portuguese data. Section~5 reports results.
Section~6 discusses the findings and the model's limitations. Section~7
concludes.

\section{Related work}

The theoretical foundations implemented here are classical: the
reproduction schemes of \citet{marx1976capital1}, the tendency of the rate
of profit discussed in \citet{marx1981capital3}, and the transformation of
values into prices of production, for which we adopt the Sraffian
linear-production-price formulation of \citet{sraffa1960production}.
\citet{okishio1961technical} established the conditions under which
technical change and the choice of technique affect the profit rate, a
result the package's technical-matrix machinery is built to be consistent
with, though we do not implement an explicit choice-of-technique search.
On the empirical side, \citet{shaikh2016capitalism} and
\citet{dumenil1993economics} are the standard references for long-run
profit-rate estimation from national accounts; our approach differs mainly
in emphasizing a fully automated, re-runnable data pipeline over manual
series construction. Closer in spirit is the strand of empirical Marxian
work that estimates the profit rate and its components econometrically
from official national-accounts series over long time spans
\citep{basu2013reserve}, and the broader program of Marxian estimation
from harmonized international input-output databases such as the World
Input-Output Database; MarxKapital shares the latter's emphasis on a
real technical matrix rather than a value-only aggregate calibration,
but differs in coupling that matrix to explicit accumulation dynamics
rather than using it for static decomposition alone. The monetary-expression-of-labor-time
problem we flag in Section~5.3 is treated at length in
\citet{freeman1996marx}. The exact quantity-rationing mechanism introduced in
Section~3.3 draws on the disequilibrium-macroeconomics literature on
short-side rationing rules, in particular \citet{barro1971general} and
\citet{benassy1975neokeynesian}, applied here to a Leontief-technology
setting rather than the labor/goods markets those papers originally
analyze.

\section{The model}

\subsection{The classical two-department scheme}

The package's starting point is Marx's classical two-department scheme:
Department~I produces means of production, Department~II produces
consumption goods. Each department $d\in\{1,2\}$ is characterized by
constant capital $C_d$, variable capital $V_d$, and a rate of exploitation
$e_d$, giving surplus value $S_d = e_d V_d$, organic composition
$k_d = C_d/V_d$, and profit rate $r_d = e_d/(k_d+1)$. Accumulation follows
\begin{align}
  \dot C_d &= \sigma_{c,d} S_d - \delta_d C_d, &
  \dot V_d &= \sigma_{v,d} S_d,
\end{align}
where $\sigma_{c,d}+\sigma_{v,d}+\gamma_d=1$ splits surplus value between
reinvestment in constant capital, reinvestment in variable capital, and
capitalist consumption, and $\delta_d$ is a depreciation rate. Throughout,
$C_d$ denotes constant capital \emph{advanced} --- a stock, the state
variable accumulating in the equation above, on which organic composition
and profit rate are conventionally computed. This is distinct from the
constant capital \emph{transferred} to output during a single period,
$\delta_dC_d$ under the model's single depreciation rate. An earlier
version of this paper called $W_d=\delta_dC_d+V_d+S_d$ ``gross output'';
that label was not accurate, since the model tracks only \emph{fixed}
constant capital $C_d$ (a depreciating stock) and has no separate state
for \emph{circulating} constant capital $z_d$ --- raw materials, energy,
purchased services, and other intermediate inputs consumed within the
period without being capitalized as a stock. True gross output is
$x_d=z_d+W_d$. Rather than leave $z_d$ unrepresented, we recover it from
the model's own cost structure: since $A_{id}$ is defined as input $i$
per unit of \emph{gross output} of sector $d$ (Section~3.2), a sector's
circulating consumption is $z_d=\bigl(\sum_iA_{id}\bigr)x_d$, so
\begin{equation}
  x_d = z_d + W_d = \Bigl(\textstyle\sum_iA_{id}\Bigr)x_d + W_d
  \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\;
  x_d = \frac{W_d}{1-\sum_iA_{id}},
  \label{eq:output-bruto}
\end{equation}
solved without introducing new state: $x_d$ is reconstructed at every
solver evaluation from $W_d$ (itself a function of the state
$C_d,V_d$) and the fixed matrix $A$, the same way Sraffa prices are
recomputed at every evaluation rather than tracked as state
(Section~3.2). Equation~\eqref{eq:output-bruto} is valid only if $A$
represents each sector's \emph{complete} circulating-input requirement
--- true by construction for the closed synthetic economies
(\texttt{criar\_matriz\_tecnica}) used in the simulations of
Section~3.3 and Section~5.4, where every input a sector uses is, by
definition, supplied by some sector inside the same $N\times N$ matrix.
It is \emph{not} generally true of the empirical technical matrix
(Section~4.2), which we deliberately build from domestic-use flows only
and which therefore, by definition, omits imported intermediate inputs;
applying Equation~\eqref{eq:output-bruto} to that matrix would
understate reconstructed output, because imported intermediate
requirements are omitted from the domestic column sum entirely; writing
$a_j^D=\sum_iA^D_{ij}$ and $a_j^M=\sum_iA^M_{ij}$ for the domestic and
imported input shares, the true identity is
$W_j=(1-a_j^D-a_j^M)x_j$, so reconstructing with only $A^D$ gives
$\hat x_j=W_j/(1-a_j^D)=x_j(1-a_j^D-a_j^M)/(1-a_j^D)$ --- an
understatement of $x_j-\hat x_j=a_j^M x_j/(1-a_j^D)$, not simply
$a_j^Mx_j$; the bias depends jointly on the domestic and imported input
shares, not on the imported share alone. We do not do this: Section~4's empirical
calibration uses Eurostat's own published $x_i$ directly and never
invokes Equation~\eqref{eq:output-bruto}, which is used exclusively for
the closed synthetic economies of Section~3.3 and Section~5.4, where the
completeness condition holds by construction.

The admissibility condition $\sum_iA_{id}<1$ for every $d$ (required for
the denominator in Equation~\eqref{eq:output-bruto} to be positive) is
not guaranteed by $\rho(A)<1$, which bounds the matrix's spectral radius
but not any individual column sum. We do not clamp this silently inside
the per-evaluation calculation; a synthetic matrix's column sums are
checked once, when the simulation is assembled, and any matrix that
violates the condition is rescaled uniformly (the same rule already used
to enforce $\rho(A)<1$) until its largest column sum equals exactly
$0.95$ (the same margin used throughout the codebase for this purpose),
with a warning logged --- an explicit admissibility rule applied
once to the matrix, not a numerical safeguard reapplied at every solver
step. Across the 270 random synthetic matrices used in the convergence
sweep of Section~5.4 ($N\in\{6,12,19\}$, density $\in\{0.2,0.4,0.6\}$),
50 ($18.5\%$) required rescaling, by a factor averaging $0.81$ (worst
case $0.63$) --- a non-negligible fraction, reported here rather than
left as an abstract possibility. We also checked whether rescaling
itself measurably changes downstream behavior, rather than assuming it
is neutral: the rescaled and non-rescaled subsets both converge in every
trial ($50/50$ and $220/220$), with the rescaled subset taking modestly
more iterations on average ($28.4$ vs.\ $25.3$) and showing a higher
mean fraction of sectors rationed ($1.00$ vs.\ $0.90$). Because
rescaling is triggered by a matrix's pre-existing column sums, the two
subsets already differ structurally by construction, and this
descriptive comparison does not identify a causal effect of rescaling
itself --- we did not separately measure matrix density (as distinct
from coefficient magnitude) between the subsets, so we do not assert
which of the two drives the larger column sums that trigger rescaling.
Section~3.3's coupling mechanisms use
$x_d$, not $W_d$, as the supply-side quantity --- resolving, rather than
merely disclosing, the gap between a value-added-type flow and genuine
gross output that an earlier version of this paper left as an open
limitation. Intersectoral
consistency (does Department~I's wage and unaccumulated-surplus income
suffice to buy Department~II's output, and vice versa) is checked
\emph{a posteriori} by a dedicated consistency function, not imposed
inside the differential system --- a design choice the $N$-department
generalization inherits and, in one respect, improves upon
(Section~3.3).

\subsection{Generalization to $N$ departments and the technical matrix}

For $N$ sectors, the model introduces a technical matrix $A\in\mathbb
R^{N\times N}_{\ge 0}$, with $A_{ij}$ the value of good $i$ required as
input per unit of output of sector $j$, constructed either synthetically
(with structural options for random, triangular, or block sparsity
patterns, always rescaled to spectral radius below unity) or --- our
empirical contribution --- from a real input-output table
(Section~4). Given $A$ and a labor-coefficient vector $l$, labor values
follow the Leontief inverse, $\lambda = (I-A)^{-1}l$, and Sraffian
production prices follow
\begin{equation}
  p = w(1+\bar r)\left[I-(1+\bar r)A^\top\right]^{-1} l,
  \label{eq:sraffa}
\end{equation}
valid for any uniform profit rate $\bar r$ below the maximum feasible rate
$R_{\max} = 1/\lambda_{\max}(A^\top) - 1$, where $\lambda_{\max}$ denotes
the dominant eigenvalue. The baseline $N$-department accumulation system
treats sectors as independent, exactly mirroring the two-department
equations sector by sector; $A$ is available to the value/price apparatus
above but does not, in this baseline system, feed back into the
accumulation dynamics themselves.

\subsection{Coupling sectors through $A$: three mechanisms}

The baseline independence of sectors is a known limitation of purely
value-accounting reproduction models: physically, one sector's constant
capital is another sector's output, and the technical matrix should
constrain how much investment a sector can realize, not merely how its
value and price magnitudes are computed. MarxKapital implements three
progressively more demanding mechanisms to address this, all opt-in and
mutually composable except where noted, applied to a coupled system whose
accumulation equation for constant capital becomes
\begin{equation}
  \dot C_i = \kappa_i\,\sigma_{c,i} S_i - \delta_i C_i +
             \mu\,(r_i - \bar r)\, C_i,
  \label{eq:coupled}
\end{equation}
where $r_i$ is computed with constant capital valued at Sraffa production
prices (using the current aggregate profit rate, clamped below
$0.95\,R_{\max}$ to avoid the pricing singularity, with a labor-value
fallback if the price system is ill-conditioned) rather than raw book
value, so that $A$ enters the dynamics through valuation as well as
through the mechanisms below.

\paragraph{Capital mobility.} The term $\mu(r_i-\bar r)C_i$ reallocates
investment toward above-average-profit sectors, a direct computational
analogue of capital's tendency to flow toward higher returns. An earlier
draft used $\bar r=\sum_iS_i/\sum_i(C_i+V_i)$, the aggregate profit-rate
ratio, for this purpose. That choice does not generally satisfy
$\sum_i\mu(r_i-\bar r)C_i=0$, since each $r_i$'s own denominator includes
$V_i$ as well as $C_i$; the term could therefore create or destroy
aggregate constant capital rather than purely reallocating it, a defect
identified by external review. We fixed this by defining $\bar r$ as the
$C_i$-weighted mean of the sectoral $r_i$ instead,
$\bar r=\sum_ir_iC_i/\sum_iC_i$ --- the only choice for which
$\sum_i\mu(r_i-\bar r)C_i=0$ holds by construction, which we verify
directly (to floating-point precision, in the model's test suite) rather
than merely asserting algebraically.

\paragraph{Aggregate realization pressure.} Setting $\kappa_i \equiv
\min(\rho_{\text{real}},1)$ uniformly across sectors, with
$\rho_{\text{real}} = \sum_i \min(O_i,(AO)_i) / \sum_i (AO)_i$ and $O_i=x_i$
the reconstructed gross output of Equation~\eqref{eq:output-bruto}
(Section~3.1) --- not the value-added-type flow $W_i$ an earlier version
of this paper used here --- dampens planned reinvestment economy-wide
whenever aggregate intermediate demand implied by $A$ and current outputs
exceeds what those same outputs can supply. Because $O_i$ is now genuine
gross output, $(AO)_i$ is dimensionally the correct intermediate demand
implied by current production, not an approximation built on an
understated supply figure. This mechanism is a coarse,
aggregate approximation --- it does not distinguish which sectors are
actually constrained --- but requires no iterative solution.

\paragraph{Exact per-sector quantity rationing.} The paper's central
technical contribution replaces the aggregate heuristic with a genuine
per-sector rationing scheme --- ``exact'' in the sense of solving the
specified proportional short-side rationing rule exactly via its fixed
point, not in the sense of a full, empirically validated model of
sectoral reproduction balances (the remaining simplifying assumption is
stated below and revisited in Section~6.2). Under Leontief technology, a sector's planned
reinvestment cannot substitute a shortage of one input with more of
another; realized investment is bounded by the scarcest required input,
and a sector's own shortfall reduces its effective demand for \emph{all}
its inputs, which in turn affects the rationing faced by its suppliers.
This makes the problem simultaneous. Writing $\rho_i\in(0,1]$ for the
rationing factor faced by \emph{suppliers} of good $i$ and $\kappa_j$ for
the realized fraction of planned reinvestment for \emph{buyer} $j$, the
fixed point is
\begin{align}
  \kappa_j &= \min_{i:\,A_{ij}>0} \rho_i, &
  D_i &= \sum_j A_{ij}\,\kappa_j\,\Delta x_j, &
  \rho_i &= \min\!\left(1, \frac{x_i}{D_i}\right), \label{eq:rationing}
\end{align}
where $\Delta x_j=\sigma_{c,j}S_j\cdot x_j/C_j$ is the \emph{desired
expansion in gross output} implied by planned investment expenditure
$\sigma_{c,j}S_j$, converted via sector $j$'s current capital-output
ratio $C_j/x_j$. An earlier version of this paper applied $A_{ij}$
directly to investment expenditure $\sigma_{c,j}S_j$, implicitly
treating a unit of investment spending as if it required the same input
mix as a unit of physical output; that conflated a value flow with a
production-scale variable, a dimensional problem external review
correctly identified as more serious than a disclosed simplification.
$\Delta x_j$ resolves it: $A_{ij}$ now multiplies an output-scale
quantity, consistent with $A$'s own definition (input $i$ per unit of
gross output of $j$), and the supply side $x_i$ is genuine reconstructed
gross output (Equation~\eqref{eq:output-bruto}), not the value-added-type
$W_i$. One simplification remains, disclosed rather than resolved: the
\emph{full} $x_i$ is treated as available to meet investment demand
$D_i$, though in reality output must also replace intermediate inputs
already counted in $z_i$ and meet consumption and other final demand
first. Since no data source in the current pipeline provides
sector-of-origin final-demand allocation, we do not net a consumption
and replacement residual out of $x_i$ before comparing it to $D_i$; the
mechanism therefore represents an upper bound on investable supply,
constraining the investment-goods side of reproduction specifically, not
full sectoral reproduction balances (Section~6.2). The calibration in
Section~4, separately, builds $A$
from Eurostat's real product flows and uses the table's own published
gross output $x_i$ directly (Section~4.2) --- Equation~\eqref{eq:output-bruto}'s
reconstruction applies only to the simulated coupling mechanisms
of this section, not to the empirical technical matrix itself.

We solve this by damped (under-relaxed) fixed-point iteration inside the
right-hand side of the ODE at every solver evaluation, in exactly the same
spirit as the Sraffa-price linear solve above, rather than exposing
$(\rho,\kappa)$ as differential-algebraic state to the solver. This choice
is deliberate: a literal DAE formulation (mass-matrix or index reduction)
or a general nonlinear-complementarity solver would add real numerical
risk and a new package dependency for no benefit over the fixed point,
which we verified converges reliably (a closed-form two-sector example
with a known solution $\rho_1=\sqrt{0.8}$, and no convergence failures
across a systematic sweep of 270 random trials spanning $N\in\{6,12,19\}$
and matrix densities $\in\{0.2,0.4,0.6\}$, deliberately scaled to force
genuine rationing rather than the trivial case, converging in a mean of
$25.8$ and a maximum of $30$ iterations against a cap of 200 ---
Section~5.4).
We emphasize that undamped iteration is \emph{not} reliable here: we
verified by hand that a small two-sector example oscillates indefinitely
between two states without under-relaxation, a concrete instance of the
well-known failure mode of naive Picard iteration on $\min$-based maps.

\paragraph{Mathematical structure of the fixed point.} The convergence
claim above is empirical, but three structural facts about the map in
Equation~\eqref{eq:rationing}, $F:\rho\mapsto\rho(D(\kappa(\rho)))$, can be
established directly rather than left as an unexplained regularity. The
natural first instinct --- following the spectral-radius condition
$\rho(A)<1$ that already governs the linear Leontief/Sraffa system of
Section~3.2 --- is to look for an analogous Banach-contraction argument
for $F$. This does not transfer: $\rho(A)<1$ bounds a different, linear
map, and, as we show below, it does not by itself bound $F$'s local
behavior. What can be established is more specific to $F$'s own
structure.

\emph{Existence is unconditional.} $D_i$ is clamped away from zero
($\max(D_i,\varepsilon)$ for a small $\varepsilon>0$, avoiding division
by zero when a sector's rationed suppliers deliver no input at all), and
$\kappa_j$ is defined as $1$ when $j$'s input column is empty (no
supplier to be rationed by). With these two conventions stated
explicitly, $\kappa$, $D$, $\rho(\cdot)$ are continuous on $[0,1]^N$, so
$F$ is a continuous self-map of this compact convex set. Brouwer's
fixed-point theorem therefore guarantees \emph{a} fixed point exists for
every admissible $(A,\sigma_c,S,x)$, independent of whether any
particular iterative scheme converges to it. Existence does not imply
uniqueness: because $F$ is antitone (below), the model could in
principle admit multiple fixed points or a genuine two-cycle rather than
converging under damping. We do not have a uniqueness proof; what we
have is a direct empirical check, run against a real 19-sector matrix
under a synthetic demand shock tight enough to bind every sector
($\kappa_i<1$ for all $i$), varying the initial $\rho$ (50 uniform draws
on $(0.1,1]^N$), the sector ordering (20 permutations, unpermuted after
solving), and the convergence tolerance ($10^{-4}$ to $10^{-12}$): the
damped iteration reached the same fixed point to numerical precision
($<10^{-6}$) in every case. This is evidence of practical uniqueness in
the regime we test, not a proof of uniqueness in general.

\emph{$F$ is antitone (order-reversing) in $\rho$.} $\kappa_j(\rho)=
\min_{i:A_{ij}>0}\rho_i$ is isotone (a minimum is monotone in each
argument); $D_i(\kappa)=\sum_jA_{ij}\kappa_j\Delta x_j$ is isotone
(a nonnegative linear combination); and $D\mapsto\min(1,x_i/D_i)$ is
antitone (decreasing in $D_i$). Composing two isotone maps with one
antitone map yields an antitone map. This is the structural reason
undamped Picard iteration oscillates rather than diverging or slowly
converging: iterating an antitone map is a setting in which two-cycles,
rather than convergence, are a characteristic possible failure mode,
because $F\circ F$ --- not $F$ itself --- is isotone. Our two-sector
example instantiates this known qualitative behavior of order-reversing
maps; it is not evidence that every antitone map in this class
oscillates, only that oscillation is a real and structurally-explained
possibility rather than an unexplained curiosity.

\emph{Damping relocates a real eigenvalue, exactly.} At an interior
fixed point $\rho^*$ (no $\rho_i$ pinned at a boundary), differentiating
Equation~\eqref{eq:rationing} gives Jacobian entries
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial F_i}{\partial\rho_k} =
  -\frac{x_i}{D_i^2}\sum_{j:\,i^*(j)=k} A_{ij}\,\Delta x_j \;\le\; 0,
\end{equation}
where $i^*(j)$ denotes a supplier attaining the minimum defining
$\kappa_j$ --- confirming antitonicity locally, in agreement with the
global argument above. For the damped step $\rho\mapsto(1-\alpha)\rho+
\alpha F(\rho)$, a real Jacobian eigenvalue $\lambda$ of $F$ at $\rho^*$
maps to $\mu(\alpha)=(1-\alpha)+\alpha\lambda$, and
$\alpha^\star=1/(1-\lambda)$ sets $\mu(\alpha^\star)=0$: damping at
$\alpha^\star$ exactly annihilates the local linear error mode
associated with $\lambda$. This is a statement about one eigenvalue, not
a claim that the full damped iteration converges superlinearly in
general --- that would additionally require every other eigenvalue of
the local Jacobian to vanish or to already lie well inside the unit
disc at $\alpha^\star$, conditions we have not checked in general. This is not hypothetical: for the closed-form
two-sector example above, the Jacobian collapses to a single active
eigenvalue $\lambda=-1$ exactly (verified both algebraically from the
formula above and numerically), for which
$\alpha^\star=1/(1-(-1))=0.5$ --- precisely the relaxation value the
package uses by default, not a value tuned after the fact to make this
example converge.

We also checked directly whether a global, $\rho(A)$-style Lipschitz
bound exists for $F$, rather than assuming one does not. Write
$L_i=\sum_jA_{ij}\Delta x_j$ for the full (unrationed) demand on
good $i$ and $r_i=x_i/L_i$. A global Lipschitz constant for $F$ on the
box $[\rho_{\min},1]^N$ is $K=\max_ix_i/(\rho_{\min}^2L_i)=\max_ir_i/
\rho_{\min}^2$; requiring $F$ to map the box into itself forces
$\rho_{\min}\le\min_ir_i$, and at the tightest admissible choice
$\rho_{\min}=\min_ir_i$ the bound gives $K\ge1/\min_ir_i>1$ whenever any
sector is actually rationed ($r_i<1$ for some $i$) --- i.e.\ this style
of global bound is provably vacuous in exactly the regime the mechanism
is designed for. We report this as a genuine negative result rather
than omit it: no clean global contraction constant in the spirit of
$\rho(A)<1$ exists for this map by this route, which is why we rely on
the local analysis above, together with the empirical convergence sweep
reported earlier in this section (270 trials, no failures --- Section~5.4),
rather than a single global theorem.

None of the three mechanisms imposes exact market clearing at every
instant for every sector as a hard constraint solved simultaneously with
the differential state --- that would require the DAE formulation we
deliberately avoid. A dedicated diagnostic function generalizes the
two-department \emph{a posteriori} check to $N$ sectors, reconstructing
$x_i$ from the state via Equation~\eqref{eq:output-bruto} and reporting
the per-sector \emph{intermediate-use residual} $x_i - (Ax)_i$ and its
aggregate ratio at a given point in the trajectory. We do not call this
a market-clearing gap or a disequilibrium measure: in standard
input-output accounting, gross output minus modeled intermediate demand
is ordinarily positive and expected --- it is output available for
final use, exports, inventories, and every other demand category this
diagnostic does not model (Section~6.2) --- so a positive residual is
the normal case, not evidence of anything approaching balance. The
residual is informative as a relative, within-model comparison (larger
under one parameterization than another, changing over the trajectory)
and now uses a genuine gross-output $x_i$ rather than the
value-added-type $W_i$ an earlier version of this diagnostic used, but
it should not be read as a sectoral equilibrium or reproduction-balance
measure on its own.

\section{Empirical calibration}

\subsection{Data sources}

MarxKapital fetches four families of real sectoral and macroeconomic data
\citep{eurostat_nama10a64,eurostat_naio10cp1700,ameco2026}, each via an
official SDMX REST API rather than a static file:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Sectoral value added, compensation, and consumption of
    fixed capital} from Eurostat's national accounts by industry
    (\texttt{NAMA\_10\_A64}), at the 19-section NACE Rev.~2 level.
  \item \textbf{Sectoral employment and gross fixed capital formation}
    from the companion Eurostat datasets \texttt{NAMA\_10\_A64\_E} and
    \texttt{NAMA\_10\_A64\_P5}.
  \item \textbf{Sectoral net capital stock} (the default series used in
    Section~4.3) from \texttt{NAMA\_10\_NFA\_ST}, item \texttt{N11N}.
  \item \textbf{A real technical matrix} from Eurostat's symmetric
    input-output table at basic prices, product by product
    (\texttt{NAIO\_10\_CP1700}), domestic-use flows only, aggregated to
    Leontief technical coefficients reconciled directly against the
    table's own published gross-output row (Section~4.2).
  \item \textbf{Aggregate macroeconomic series} (GDP, employment,
    compensation, capital stock, and eighteen further variables) from the
    European Commission's AMECO database.
\end{itemize}

All four sources were, until recently, distributed as static bulk files
with heuristic, undocumented formats; both institutions have since
migrated to
official SDMX REST APIs, which MarxKapital uses directly, with the legacy
static-file parsers retained only as a manual fallback.

\subsection{Constructing the technical matrix: a second bug, found by auditing rather than assuming correctness}

An earlier version of this pipeline, and of this paper, built the
technical matrix from \texttt{NAIO\_10\_CP1620}. External review asked us
to audit this construction --- quantify the effect of a margin-clipping
step, reconcile row/column totals against published data, document how
product and industry classifications are matched, and resolve whether
imports are treated as domestic supply. Carrying out that audit
(\texttt{paper/matrix\_audit.jl}) surfaced something more fundamental
than any of those individual questions: \texttt{NAIO\_10\_CP1620} is not
the symmetric input-output table. Its Eurostat title is ``Table of trade
and transport margins'' --- a small satellite table used to decompose the
use table at purchasers' prices into its components (basic-price flows
plus margins plus taxes less subsidies), not the symmetric table itself.
Building $A$ from it produced flows understated by more than an order of
magnitude: for Portugal, 2020 (the same reference year as the corrected
matrix below, for a like-for-like comparison), total intermediate
consumption implied by \texttt{NAIO\_10\_CP1620} is
$\approx\mkern1mu$\texteuro8.8\,bn, against a true economy-wide figure of
$\approx\mkern1mu$\texteuro177.8\,bn (published output less published
value added, summed across all NACE sections). Every negative cell we
had previously attributed to a margin-reallocation accounting convention
was simply the mechanics of that satellite table, not a property of the
real input-output structure at all. This is the direct explanation for
the implausibly low spectral radius ($\rho(A)\approx0.13$) and
implausibly high $R_{\max}$ ($\approx691\%$) reported in earlier
versions of this paper, which the review correctly flagged as suspicious
without yet knowing why.

The real table is \texttt{NAIO\_10\_CP1700}, ``Symmetric input-output
table at basic prices (product by product).'' Rebuilding $A$ from it,
for Portugal, gives zero negative cells (not 272--317 clipped, as
before) and reconciles \emph{exactly} against the table's own published
gross-output row (item \texttt{P1}, available for all 19 sections), which
we now use directly as the output denominator rather than approximating
it from value added and intermediate-flow sums. The corrected matrix has
spectral radius $\rho(A)\approx0.52$ and implied $R_{\max}\approx91\%$
(Section~5.3) --- both economically plausible, unlike their predecessors.

Two further questions the review raised are resolved directly rather
than left as caveats. \emph{Imports:} \texttt{NAIO\_10\_CP1700} publishes
domestic-use (\texttt{DOM}), imports-use (\texttt{IMP}), and combined
(\texttt{TOTAL}) variants --- confirmed by querying the API with the
\texttt{stk\_flow} dimension unconstrained, something
\texttt{NAIO\_10\_CP1620} did not offer (it published \texttt{TOTAL}
only). We use \texttt{DOM}: $A$ is meant to represent domestic
inter-sectoral production dependence, and an imported input is not
supplied by any domestic sector, so including it in $A$ would assert a
domestic supply relationship that does not exist. \emph{Classification:}
the symmetric table is genuinely product-by-product, using the same CPA
product code on both the row (\texttt{prd\_ava}) and column
(\texttt{prd\_use}) dimensions at the resolution we use --- there is no
CPA-to-NACE concordance to document for this step, since no NACE-classified
dimension is involved in constructing $A$ itself; NACE enters only
through the separately-sourced sectoral value-added weights
(\texttt{NAMA\_10\_A64}), a different and already-documented join.

One limitation the audit surfaced and we chose to resolve by scoping,
rather than by leaving a mismatch in place: \texttt{NAIO\_10\_CP1700} is
a benchmark table, published for specific reference years (2010, 2013,
2015, 2017, 2020 for Portugal, not annually), not a series like the rest
of the pipeline. Requesting a matrix for 2022 silently falls back to the
nearest available reference year --- 2020 for Portugal, logged
explicitly as a warning at calibration time --- which would mix a
2020-vintage matrix with 2022 labor coefficients if left uncorrected. We
avoid that mismatch directly: Figures~\ref{fig:matrixA}
and~\ref{fig:transformation} (the heatmap and the transformation-problem
comparison) use a fully coherent 2020 calibration, matrix \emph{and}
labor coefficients both from 2020, not a mixed vintage. Table~\ref{tab:sectors}'s
sectoral $e$, $k$, $r$, and VAB figures do not depend on $A$ at all and
remain genuinely 2022 (Section~5.1), as does the aggregate 1996--2022
AMECO series (Section~5.2); neither needed to change. The paper's
results therefore span two internally-coherent reference points --- a
2020 production-structure snapshot (Figures~\ref{fig:matrixA}--\ref{fig:transformation})
and a 2022 sectoral/aggregate snapshot (Table~\ref{tab:sectors},
Figure~\ref{fig:timeseries}) --- rather than one section silently
combining both.

\subsection{Depreciation and capital stock}

By default the model uses the net capital stock reported directly by
Eurostat for the requested year. As an option, it instead constructs a
perpetual-inventory series, $K_t = (1-\delta)K_{t-1} + I_t$, seeded from
the earliest year with both a reported stock and investment series, with
$\delta$ estimated per sector from the ratio of consumption of fixed
capital to stock in that base year rather than assumed. This uses a
single depreciation rate per sector, not differentiated by asset type; we
return to this simplification in Section~6.2.

The aggregate AMECO capital-stock series used in Section~5.2 (item
\texttt{OKND}, ``net capital stock at 2020 prices'') is, by contrast,
already a constant-price (volume) series, whereas AMECO's wage
(\texttt{UWCD}) and output (\texttt{UVGD}) series are current-price. The
pipeline exposes both the raw combination and valuation-consistent
alternatives that deflate the wage and output series by AMECO's own GDP
deflator (\texttt{PVGD}$=$\texttt{UVGD}/\texttt{OVGD}$\times100$) or
investment-goods deflator (\texttt{PIGT}) onto the same basis as the
capital stock; Section~5.2 reports all three.

To avoid the ambiguity of an unqualified ``surplus,'' we distinguish
\begin{equation}
  S^{\text{gross}} = Y - V, \qquad
  S^{\text{net}} = Y - V - \text{CCF},
\end{equation}
and state explicitly which is used where. The invariance argument above
concerns $S^{\text{gross}}$: $(Y-V)/V$ is exactly invariant under a
common deflator applied to $Y$ and $V$, with no dependence on how CCF is
measured. The rate of exploitation $e$ and profit rate $r$ reported in
Section~5.2 and throughout the aggregate time series use
$S^{\text{net}}$ (equivalently, $e=(Y-V-\text{CCF})/V$), with CCF drawn
from AMECO's own consumption-of-fixed-capital series (item
\texttt{UKCT}, current-price, deflated consistently with $V$ and $Y$ in
each convention) rather than an assumed fraction of the capital stock;
an earlier version of this calculation substituted a flat $10\%$ of the
(constant-price) capital stock for CCF: a constant-price, unscaled term
that does not move with the deflator applied to $Y$ and $V$, which
breaks the algebraic invariance of $S^{\text{net}}/V$ specifically
(there being no CCF-like term in $S^{\text{gross}}/V=(Y-V)/V$ for a
mismeasured CCF to disturb) --- an inconsistency we found via external
review and verify is now resolved in Section~5.2, where we report that
$e^{\text{net}}$ (computed with $S^{\text{net}}$, CCF deflated
consistently with $Y$ and $V$) is invariant across conventions exactly,
confirming the fix addresses the right term. The sectoral cross-section of Section~5.1 also uses
$S^{\text{net}}$ throughout (business income after consumption of fixed
capital, Section~4.3).

Sectoral estimates of $e$ (Section~5.1) impose a floor of $0.1$: a small
number of sectors have business income, net of consumption of fixed
capital, close to zero or negative relative to compensation, which the
model's positivity requirement on $e$ (Section~3.1) does not allow. This
is an imposed value, not a purely empirical one, and the pipeline now
reports explicitly how many sectors hit it (Section~5.1) rather than
leaving the floor invisible in the reported figures.

\section{Results: Portugal}

\subsection{Sectoral profile, 2022}

Throughout this section and Section~5.2, $e$ and $r$ are computed from
national-accounts aggregates (Section~4.3) and should be read as a
\emph{national-accounts proxy} for the theoretical rate of exploitation
and profit rate, not a direct measurement of either. Business income net
of consumption of fixed capital, the numerator in our construction, is
not a clean measure of surplus value: it is not adjusted for mixed
income of the self-employed (allocated to neither $V$ nor $S$ here),
taxes less subsidies on production, imputed rents, non-market output, or
other national-accounts conventions that do not map cleanly onto Marxian
categories. This matters most for exactly the sectors where these
conventions bite hardest --- real estate, agriculture, public
administration, education, and health --- and the real-estate outlier
discussed below is one visible symptom of it, not the only one. We do
not attempt gross/net or mixed-income-adjusted variants of the sectoral
profile in this paper; doing so properly is future work.

Table~\ref{tab:sectors} reports the calibrated rate of exploitation, organic composition,
profit rate, and gross value added (VAB, sourced directly from the
underlying Eurostat data, not reconstructed from model parameters) for
all 19 sections, ranked by profit rate. Sectoral VAB sums to
$\approx\mkern1mu209.8$ thousand~M\texteuro, matching Portugal's 2022
national accounts total by construction --- an accounting identity the
pipeline checks and that an earlier, buggy version of this table
violated by two orders of magnitude, because it reconstructed a stand-in
for VAB as $C_i+V_i+e_iV_i$ (adding a capital \emph{stock} to two flows,
the same conflation discussed in Section~3.1) instead of reporting the
real underlying series.

Three different summary statistics for the sectoral rates are worth
distinguishing, since they answer different questions and can diverge
substantially: the simple (unweighted) mean across the 19 sectors is
$e\approx0.89$, $k\approx24.8$, $r\approx10.0\%$; the VAB-weighted mean
is $e\approx1.45$, $k\approx44.2$, $r\approx10.7\%$; and the properly
aggregated rate, $\sum_iS_i/\sum_i(C_i+V_i)$ (summing numerators and
denominators separately before dividing, rather than averaging ratios),
is $r\approx6.1\%$. The gap between these is driven substantially by a
single outlier, real estate activities ($k\approx334$), plausibly
reflecting the well-documented effect of imputed owner-occupied rents on
this sector's national-accounts capital intensity (a compositional
effect of the accounting convention, not something we have independently
verified is the sole driver here); it should be treated with caution
when interpreting any of the three summary figures. Four of the 19 sectors
hit the $e=0.1$ floor described in Section~4.3 (business income net of
depreciation close to zero or negative relative to compensation) ---
an imposed value in those four cases, not a purely empirical
observation. Excluding real estate, sectoral profit rates range from
financial and insurance activities at the top ($r\approx36\%$) to public
administration and utilities near the bottom ($r$ below 2\%), a
dispersion consistent with the classical expectation that market prices,
not labor values, equalize profit rates across sectors of differing
organic composition --- which Section~5.3 examines directly.

\subsection{Aggregate profit-rate trend, 1996--2022}

Figure~\ref{fig:timeseries} shows the aggregate rate of exploitation, organic composition,
and profit rate for Portugal over 1996--2022, calibrated entirely from
real AMECO series, under two valuation conventions (Section~4.3): the
solid line combines AMECO's current-price wage and output series with its
constant-price capital-stock series without adjustment (\emph{current-price
V, VAB}); the dashed line first deflates the wage and output series onto
the same constant-price basis as the capital stock (\emph{deflated V,
VAB}), using AMECO's own GDP deflator, with the surplus computed from
real consumption-of-fixed-capital data (Section~4.3), not an assumed
depreciation rate. Under both conventions the rate of exploitation moves
identically, from $0.785$ in 1996 to $0.739$ in 2022 (ranging $0.68$ to
$0.91$ over the period, dipping sharply in 2020) --- \emph{exactly}
identically, as the algebra in Section~4.3 requires once $Y$, $V$, and
the depreciation deduction are deflated by a common index, and as we
verify directly (maximum difference across all 27 years: $0$, to
floating-point precision). This was not the case in an earlier, buggy
version of this calibration, where the two conventions diverged sharply
($0.21\to0.61$ vs.\ $0.58\to0.57$) --- the divergence was itself the
symptom of the flat-depreciation bug, not a property of the underlying
valuation question, and disappears once the bug is fixed. What remains
convention-sensitive is the profit rate, through the capital-advanced
denominator $C+V$: under the unadjusted convention it rises modestly
from $7.9\%$ (1996) to $11.5\%$ (2022), ranging $7.9$--$11.5\%$ over the
full period; under the GDP-deflated convention it moves from $12.6\%$ to
$10.9\%$ at the endpoints but ranges $9.3$--$12.7\%$ over the full period
(the endpoints are not the extremes); under an investment-deflator
convention (\texttt{PIGT} in place of \texttt{PVGD}, Section~6.1) from
$15.0\%$ to $12.3\%$ at the endpoints, ranging $9.3$--$15.1\%$ over the
full period. Across all three conventions and all 27 years, the profit
rate's global range is $7.9$--$15.1\%$ --- a real, moderate sensitivity to
the deflator chosen, moving mildly within each convention rather than
dramatically, and not the near-fivefold artifact the earlier, buggy
calibration reported. Figure~\ref{fig:ptvses} repeats the GDP-deflated calculation for Spain,
using the identical pipeline: the rate of exploitation again moves
identically to the unadjusted convention (verified the same way), from
$0.85$ to $0.76$, while the profit rate moves from $12.0\%$ to $9.1\%$ under
the deflated convention (vs.\ $7.9\%$ to $9.6\%$ unadjusted) --- the same
qualitative picture as Portugal: a real but modest level of
convention-sensitivity confined to the profit rate. We discuss the
implications of this in Section~6.1.

\subsection{The transformation problem on the real economy}

Figure~\ref{fig:transformation} compares labor values with Sraffian production prices computed
on the real calibrated matrix, for three empirically plausible uniform
profit rates ($\bar r=5\%,10\%,15\%$). With the corrected technical
matrix (Section~4.2), $R_{\max}\approx91\%$ for this economy --- still
higher than any plausible real profit rate, but no longer the
$\approx691\%$ an earlier, buggy version of the matrix construction
implied, and squarely in an economically interpretable range for a
maximum feasible rate. We still plot at concrete empirical rates rather
than fractions of $R_{\max}$, since $R_{\max}$ itself is not a rate any
real economy operates at; we checked that the qualitative pattern below
is unchanged at $0.3R_{\max}\approx27\%$ and $0.6R_{\max}\approx54\%$
without including a second figure. As $\bar r$ rises, sectors with above-average organic
composition see their production price rise relative to their labor
value, and vice versa, the qualitative signature the transformation

and compares relative deviations, which is sufficient to display the
sign and ordering of the transformation effect but does not license a
literal price-value comparison in a common unit --- a well-known
difficulty in empirical Marxian value theory
\citep{freeman1996marx,basu2013reserve}.

\subsection{Comparison of coupling mechanisms: one illustration, then a systematic sweep}

Because no real per-sector accumulation time series exists to calibrate
the dynamic coupling mechanisms of Section~3.3 empirically, Figure~\ref{fig:coupling}
first illustrates their qualitative behavior on a single synthetic
six-sector economy under one random technical matrix and seed. Capital
mobility alone reduces cross-sector profit-rate dispersion over time, as
expected. Adding the aggregate realization-pressure term leaves the
qualitative picture largely unchanged in this run. Adding exact quantity
rationing instead \emph{increases} final dispersion relative to mobility
alone in this particular random economy: rationing can prevent the
mobility term from fully equalizing rates when a high-profit sector's
own suppliers cannot support the investment mobility would otherwise
direct there.

An earlier draft reported this as an example of the mechanism's
behavior without evidence it generalizes beyond one random seed. We
checked directly rather than leaving that as an open question:
Figure~\ref{fig:dispersion} repeats the same three-way comparison across
$50$ independent random seeds (each with its own random six-sector
technical matrix, sectoral parameters, and initial conditions). Rationing
produces \emph{higher} final dispersion than mobility alone in
\textbf{50 of 50} seeds --- the pattern in Figure~\ref{fig:coupling} is
not an artifact of one unlucky draw, but a consistent finding across
this specific class of random six-sector economies (moderate density,
the parameter ranges of \texttt{criar\_matriz\_tecnica} and
\texttt{criar\_departamentos}); we do not claim it generalizes beyond
that tested class. The mean final dispersion is
$0.063$ (mobility only), $0.189$ (adding realization pressure), and
$0.448$ (adding rationing) --- rationing roughly triples dispersion
relative to realization pressure and roughly seven-folds it relative to
mobility alone, on average, with substantial spread across seeds
(rationing's dispersion ranges $0.168$--$0.687$ across the 50 economies).
The economic reading is the same as before, now with numerical backing:
exact per-sector rationing is a genuinely different, more disequalizing
mechanism than either capital mobility or an aggregate realization
heuristic, because it can trap a high-profit sector behind a
supply-constrained supplier in a way neither of the other two mechanisms
can represent.

We separately checked the rationing fixed point's convergence claim
(Section~3.3) far beyond the closed-form example and the earlier
twenty-parameter-combination sweep: $270$ trials spanning
$N\in\{6,12,19\}$ and matrix density $\in\{0.2,0.4,0.6\}$ (30 seeds per
combination), with supply deliberately scaled to force genuine rationing
rather than the trivial $\kappa\equiv1$ case ($268/270$ trials had at
least one rationed sector). The damped iteration converged in
\textbf{270 of 270} trials, in a mean of $25.8$ and a maximum of $30$
iterations (against a cap of 200) --- no failures, and no case requiring
anywhere near the iteration budget. This remains empirical evidence, not
a proof (Section~3.3), but it is now evidence across a systematic sweep
rather than a small hand-picked set.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{A stock-flow conflation and a valuation artifact}

A first pass at the raw AMECO series appears to show a profit rate rising
sharply for Portugal over 1996--2022, driven by an organic composition
that falls even as the rate of exploitation nearly triples. This is the
pattern we originally reported. It does not survive a closer look, and
tracing why it does not survive turned out to require finding two
separate defects, not one.

\emph{The first defect was in the model, not the data.} Gross output
throughout MarxKapital was computed as $O_i=C_i+V_i+S_i$ (Section~3.3),
adding a capital \emph{stock} to two annual \emph{flows}; the aggregate
AMECO calculation used the same conflation implicitly, deducting a flat
$10\%$ of the (constant-price) capital stock as ``depreciation'' from a
current-price surplus. External review of an earlier draft caught this.
It matters here specifically because the flat-depreciation term breaks
an algebraic identity that should otherwise hold for the quantity
actually reported. The rate of exploitation used throughout this paper
is net of consumption of fixed capital, $e^{\text{net}}=(Y-V-\text{CCF})/V$
(Section~4.3); if $Y$, $V$, and CCF are \emph{all} deflated by a common
price index, $e^{\text{net}}$ is invariant under that deflation --- the
common factor cancels from every term. The earlier, buggy version
deducted a flat $10\%$ of the (constant-price) capital stock in place of
CCF: an unscaled term that does \emph{not} move with the deflator
applied to $Y$ and $V$, so it breaks precisely the invariance
$e^{\text{net}}$ should have, and breaks it in a way that depends on how
much the deflator itself has moved --- which changes over 26 years of
cumulative inflation, producing not just a level shift between
conventions but a diverging \emph{trend}. This, not a genuine valuation
difference, is what generated the original $e:0.21\to0.61$ figure. (The
purely gross quantity $S^{\text{gross}}/V=(Y-V)/V$, which never involved
CCF at all, was never the source of the problem; it is trivially
invariant under any common deflator regardless of this bug, since no
unscaled term ever entered it.)

We fixed the underlying conflation directly (Section~3.1, Section~4.3):
output-facing formulas now use $\delta_iC_i$ (capital \emph{transferred}
in a period) rather than $C_i$ (capital \emph{advanced}, a stock), and
the aggregate calculation now uses AMECO's own consumption-of-fixed-capital
series (properly deflated alongside $Y$ and $V$) rather than an assumed
rate. The predicted consequence is
checkable, and we checked it directly rather than asserting it: with the
fix, $e^{\text{net}}$ is now \emph{exactly} identical between the unadjusted
and GDP-deflated conventions for every year in the 1996--2022 series,
for both Portugal and Spain (Section~5.2). This is not a small
improvement in agreement --- it is exact equality to floating-point
precision, which is what the algebra predicts once every term shares a
common deflation factor, and is the strongest kind
of internal-consistency check a reader could ask for: not ``the numbers
look more reasonable now,'' but ``the two conventions are now provably
required to agree, and they do.''

\emph{The second, genuine question is the one we originally set out to
answer:} does AMECO's capital stock (constant-price) versus wage and
output series (current-price) distort the measured profit rate once the
depreciation bug is out of the way? It does, but modestly. Portugal's
profit rate now ranges $7.9\%$--$15.1\%$ across three deflation
conventions over the full period (Section~5.2) --- rising mildly from
$7.9\%$ to $11.5\%$ unadjusted, declining mildly at the endpoints from
$12.6\%$ to $10.9\%$ (ranging $9.3$--$12.7\%$ across the full period)
under the GDP deflator, and from $15.0\%$ to $12.3\%$ at the endpoints
(ranging $9.3$--$15.1\%$) under an investment-goods deflator. We checked the investment-deflator variant
specifically because AMECO's capital stock is a chain-linked series built
from investment flows using an investment-specific price index
(\texttt{PIGT}), not the general GDP deflator (\texttt{PVGD}); the two
give different \emph{levels} but the same qualitative shape (mild
decline), evidence the residual sensitivity is not an artifact of which
deflator was picked. Applying the GDP-deflated convention to Spain
(Figure~\ref{fig:ptvses}) shows the same modest pattern: a profit rate
moving from $12.0\%$ to $9.1\%$ against $7.9\%$ to $9.6\%$ unadjusted. We
do not read either country's series as proof of a general tendential
pattern from a two-country, single-source comparison; national-accounts
profit-rate estimates remain sensitive to depreciation conventions and
sectoral composition, discussed further in Section~6.2. We also could
not find, and do not believe AMECO publishes, a current-price
(replacement-cost) capital-stock series that would let us skip deflation
entirely and compare all three quantities at a common current-year
valuation, which would be the cleanest check in principle.

We report the full sequence --- the original artifact, the stock-flow bug
that substantially manufactured it, the fix, and the smaller genuine
sensitivity that remains --- rather than only the corrected numbers,
because the sequence is the more durable methodological finding here: an
automated pipeline that conflates a stock with a flow, or mixes
valuation conventions across variables pulled from the same database,
can each independently manufacture a statistically salient but spurious
trend, and the two can compound. Disaggregating the corrected aggregate
trend by sector, and comparing it against the 2022 cross-section of
Table~\ref{tab:sectors}, is beyond the scope of this paper ---
complicated further by the single-year nature of the sectoral
calibration versus the time-series nature of the aggregate result ---
and is left for future work.

\subsection{Limitations}

We report limitations transparently, following the same practice as the
package's own documentation.

\emph{Default behavior remains synthetic.} Both calibration paths default
to synthetic data or fixed sectoral weights; every real-data result in
this paper required explicit opt-in flags. This is a deliberate design
choice to keep the package's automated test suite fully offline, not a
limitation of the real-data pipeline itself, but it means synthetic
results should never be mistaken for empirical ones when using the
package's defaults.

\emph{Single-rate depreciation.} The perpetual-inventory capital-stock
option (Section~4.3) uses one depreciation rate per sector, not
differentiated by asset type. Eurostat publishes investment by asset type
for the relevant dataflow, but not the corresponding asset-specific
depreciation profiles, which would be needed for a fully differentiated
perpetual-inventory calculation.

\emph{The dynamical model's output measure was resolved; final demand
was not.} An earlier version of this paper used a value-added-type flow
$W_i=\delta_iC_i+V_i+S_i$ as the supply side of Section~3.3's coupling
mechanisms, omitting circulating constant capital (materials, energy,
purchased services) entirely. We closed this gap rather than continuing
to disclose it: Equation~\eqref{eq:output-bruto} reconstructs genuine
gross output $x_i$ from $W_i$ and the model's own cost structure at
every solver evaluation, without adding new state, and both coupling
mechanisms now use $x_i$. The investment-demand side was fixed
correspondingly (Equation~\eqref{eq:rationing}): $A_{ij}$ now multiplies
a desired \emph{output} expansion $\Delta x_j$, converted from planned
investment expenditure via the sector's own capital-output ratio, not
raw expenditure directly. One gap remains, disclosed rather than
resolved: the quantity-rationing mechanism still treats the \emph{full}
reconstructed $x_i$ as available to meet investment demand, though
output must also replace intermediate inputs and meet consumption and
other final demand first. Closing this fully would require an explicit
final-demand allocation across sectors, for which no data source in the
current pipeline provides sector-of-origin consumption baskets; until
then, $D_i\le x_i$ should be read as an upper bound on investable
supply, not a fully netted reproduction balance. This limitation is
narrower than before --- confined to the investable-residual question,
not the output measure itself.

\emph{Limited robustness and sensitivity analysis, partially addressed.}
We report the aggregate trend under three valuation conventions
(unadjusted, GDP-deflated, investment-deflated; Section~5.2 and
Section~6.1) and for two countries (Portugal and Spain), which is enough
to show the corrected finding is not a single-country, single-deflator
artifact, but not enough to establish a general pattern across
peripheral EU economies, nor to rule out sectoral composition effects
that an aggregate series cannot separate from a genuine average-rate
change. The sectoral profile (Table~\ref{tab:sectors}) still uses a
single set of choices: net capital stock rather than perpetual-inventory,
and a single value of the mobility coefficient $\mu$. For the coupling
mechanisms of Section~3.3, we no longer rely on a single random seed: a
270-trial sweep (Section~5.4) confirms the rationing fixed point
converges reliably ($270/270$) across varied $N$, matrix density, and
demand/supply configurations, and a 50-seed sweep confirms rationing
produces higher dispersion than mobility alone systematically
($50/50$ seeds), not as an artifact of one draw. What these sweeps do
not cover: a fixed $\mu$ and fixed relaxation parameter throughout, and
only random (not empirically calibrated) technical matrices for the
dynamic comparison, since no real per-sector accumulation series exists
to calibrate it against (Section~5.4). Section~3.3
establishes that a fixed point always exists (Brouwer), explains
structurally why undamped iteration oscillates rather than converges
(the map is antitone), gives direct empirical evidence of practical
uniqueness under variation in initial conditions, sector ordering, and
tolerance in one genuinely-rationed test case, and shows the default
damping annihilates the dominant local error mode exactly in the
closed-form two-sector case. What remains open is a uniqueness
\emph{proof} and a global convergence-\emph{rate} guarantee across all
admissible $(A,\sigma,S,O)$ and initial conditions --- and Section~3.3's
own negative result suggests the obvious route to the latter, a global
Lipschitz bound in the spirit of $\rho(A)<1$, cannot supply it: we
showed algebraically that its tightest admissible form always exceeds
$1$ whenever any sector is actually rationed. Closing this gap, if it
can be closed at all, likely requires a different technique --- possibly
exploiting the antitone structure directly (e.g.\ bounding $F\circ F$)
rather than a Banach-style argument on $F$ itself --- and we continue to
rely on the empirical evidence above for the general case in the
meantime.

\emph{The technical matrix construction was audited, and the audit found
a bigger problem than expected.} We set out to quantify a margin-clipping
step's effect, reconcile row/column totals, document the CPA-to-NACE
concordance, and resolve the domestic-vs-total-use question
(\texttt{paper/matrix\_audit.jl}). Instead we found the pipeline had been
pulling from the wrong Eurostat dataflow entirely (Section~4.2):
\texttt{NAIO\_10\_CP1620} is a trade-and-transport-margins satellite
table, not the symmetric input-output table, and building $A$ from it
understated intermediate flows by more than an order of magnitude ---
the direct cause of the implausibly low $\rho(A)\approx0.13$ and
implausibly high $R_{\max}\approx691\%$ this paper previously reported,
and of every negative cell we had attributed to a margin-accounting
convention. Rebuilt from the correct table (\texttt{NAIO\_10\_CP1700}),
the matrix has zero negative cells, reconciles \emph{exactly} against
the table's own published output row for all 19 sections, and gives
$\rho(A)\approx0.52$, $R_{\max}\approx91\%$ --- both economically
plausible. The domestic-vs-total-use question is also resolved rather
than left open: the correct table publishes a genuine domestic-use
variant, which we use, directly addressing the concern that imported
inputs were being modeled as domestic supply. The CPA-to-NACE
concordance question does not require a documented mapping \emph{inside}
the matrix construction step itself: the symmetric table's two
dimensions (\texttt{prd\_ava}, \texttt{prd\_use}) both use the same CPA
product code, so $A$ is built entirely within one classification, with
no product-to-industry join. A concordance assumption is still made one
level up, where $A$'s CPA-labeled sections are combined with
NACE-classified labor coefficients (\texttt{NAMA\_10\_A64}) for the
production-structure analysis of Figures~\ref{fig:matrixA}--\ref{fig:transformation}.
Table~\ref{tab:sectors} is constructed independently, directly from
NACE-classified sectoral accounts, and does not depend on $A$ at all
(Section~5.1). We rely on
Eurostat's standard design correspondence between CPA and NACE section
codes at this level of aggregation (both derive from the same top-level
classification structure) rather than an independently verified
concordance table, and label $A$'s axes as CPA product sections, not
NACE industry sections, to keep this distinction visible. What remains
unresolved, and cannot be fixed by writing more code:
\texttt{NAIO\_10\_CP1700} is a benchmark table published for specific
reference years (2010, 2013, 2015, 2017, 2020 for Portugal), not
annually. We resolved the resulting vintage question by scoping rather
than by leaving a silent mismatch: Figures~\ref{fig:matrixA}
and~\ref{fig:transformation} now use a fully coherent 2020 calibration
(matrix and labor coefficients both 2020), while Table~\ref{tab:sectors}
and the aggregate AMECO series remain genuinely 2022, since neither
depends on $A$. The paper's empirical results therefore span two
internally-coherent reference points rather than one section silently
mixing 2020 and 2022 data (Section~4.2). An
explicit accounting-reconciliation assertion in the pipeline (failing
loudly if a sector's computed output does not match the table's own
\texttt{P1} row, in the spirit of the VAB check in Section~5.1) would be
a reasonable next hardening step, though the reconciliation is already
exact in the cases we checked.

\emph{The mobility term's conservation was fixed} (Section~3.3): an
earlier draft's $\bar r$ did not guarantee $\sum_i\mu(r_i-\bar r)C_i=0$;
redefining $\bar r$ as the $C_i$-weighted mean of $r_i$ makes this hold
by construction, verified directly rather than assumed. \emph{The
rationing mechanism's demand term was also fixed}: an earlier draft
applied $A_{ij}$ directly to planned investment \emph{expenditure}
$\sigma_{c,j}S_j$, as if a unit of investment spending required the same
input mix as a unit of physical output. Section~3.3's
$\Delta x_j=\sigma_{c,j}S_j\cdot x_j/C_j$ resolves this by converting
expenditure to an output-scale quantity via the sector's own
capital-output ratio before $A_{ij}$ is applied to it. One simplification
remains, disclosed rather than resolved: the full reconstructed $x_i$ is
still treated as available for investment demand $D_i$ before
intermediate replacement (already counted in $z_i$) and consumption and
other final demand are met --- overstating investable supply, since no
data source in the current pipeline provides sector-of-origin
consumption baskets to net out. $D_i\le x_i$ should therefore be read as
an upper bound on investable supply, not a fully netted reproduction
balance (Section~3.3).

\emph{Reproducibility is live-query reproducibility, not archival
reproducibility.} Every result in this paper is produced by re-running
\texttt{gerar\_resultados.jl} against the live Eurostat and AMECO SDMX
APIs (Availability, below), which is re-runnable but not immune to
retroactive revisions in the underlying series, dataset vintages, or API
structure --- a live query today is not guaranteed to reproduce a number
reported here indefinitely. A stronger reproducibility architecture
would pair a tagged package release with cached raw API responses (or a
frozen archival snapshot, e.g.\ on Zenodo) alongside the live-query path,
letting a reader choose between reproducing this paper's exact reported
numbers from frozen data and refreshing the analysis against current
data; we have not built this yet.

\section{Conclusion}

MarxKapital demonstrates that a real, live, officially sourced data
pipeline for Marxian value-theoretic calibration is practical using
current European statistical infrastructure, and that an exact per-sector
quantity-rationing mechanism --- solving a genuine fixed point, though
not imposing hard market clearing at every instant (Section~3.3) --- can
be added to a reproduction-schema model without the numerical overhead
of a formal differential-algebraic formulation. Applied to Portugal and
Spain, the pipeline surfaces --- and lets us diagnose and fix --- two
distinct measurement problems: a stock-flow conflation in the model's
own output formula, caught by external review, and a resulting
valuation artifact in the aggregate profit-rate series that we had
originally overstated because of it. Corrected, the underlying trend is
real but modest --- a $7.9\%$--$15.1\%$ range for Portugal across three
deflation conventions, not the near-fivefold swing an earlier, buggy
version of this calibration reported. Two further defects surfaced by
the same review process are also fixed and verified directly rather than
merely asserted: the capital-mobility term now conserves aggregate
constant capital exactly by construction, and the rationing mechanism's
convergence and dispersion claims (Section~5.4) now rest on systematic
sweeps of 270 and 50 random economies respectively, not a single
hand-picked illustration. A fourth audit, of the technical matrix
itself, found the most serious defect of the four: the pipeline had been
using the wrong Eurostat dataflow, understating intermediate flows by
more than an order of magnitude and producing an implausible spectral
radius and maximum profit rate that this paper had, in an earlier
version, reported without knowing why they looked wrong. Rebuilt from
the correct table, the matrix reconciles exactly against its own
published output figures and gives economically plausible values
throughout (Section~4.2, Section~6.2). A fifth defect, in the dynamical
model itself, was resolved rather than only disclosed: the coupling
mechanisms' supply side omitted circulating constant capital entirely,
and their investment-demand side applied the technical matrix to
expenditure rather than output. Both are fixed by reconstructing genuine
gross output from the model's own state at every solver evaluation
(Section~3.1, Equation~\eqref{eq:output-bruto}) and converting planned
investment expenditure to an output-scale quantity via each sector's
capital-output ratio before applying $A$ to it (Section~3.3) --- neither
requires new state or a heavier solver. We think this exercise is itself a
useful illustration of what a live, multi-source statistical pipeline
buys a computational Marxian model: not just convenience, but the
ability to catch exactly this kind of measurement and modeling error
before it is mistaken for an economic finding --- provided the pipeline
is checked as carefully as the theory it implements, and provided
implausible intermediate results (a spectral radius an order of
magnitude too low, in this case) are investigated rather than reported
with a caveat and left for later. Extending the
valuation-consistent comparison to more economies and archiving frozen data
snapshots for stronger reproducibility guarantees (Section~6.2) are left
for future work.
